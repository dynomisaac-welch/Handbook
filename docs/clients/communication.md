# Client Communication Standards

How we communicate with clients is as important as what we build for them. Sloppy communication erodes trust faster than a missed deadline. These standards exist so every client has a consistent, professional experience regardless of which DM or engineer they're talking to.

---

## Core Principles

**Proactive over reactive.** Don't wait for the client to ask for an update. If something changed, something slipped, or something came up — reach out. Clients who feel they have to chase us for information lose confidence fast.

**One voice on the account.** The Delivery Manager is the primary client contact. Engineers and other team members can communicate directly with client counterparts on day-to-day technical matters, but anything involving scope, timeline, budget, or escalation goes through the DM. Clients should never hear conflicting messages from different people on our team.

**Write it down.** If a decision is made in a meeting or a call, it gets confirmed in writing — in an email, a status update, or a meeting recap — within 24 hours. Verbal agreements have a short shelf life.

**No surprises.** A client should never hear bad news for the first time in a weekly report. If something significant is happening, loop them in immediately. The report confirms and summarizes; it doesn't break news.

---

## Communication Channels

We use specific channels for specific purposes. Don't blur the lines — using the wrong channel for the wrong message creates confusion and undermines professionalism.

**Email** is the primary record of client communication. Project status updates, decisions, scope discussions, and anything that needs to be documented goes to email. Response time: within 24 hours during business days, within 4 hours for anything flagged urgent.

**Video calls (Zoom or Teams)** are for discussions that benefit from real-time dialogue — kickoffs, demos, discovery sessions, and escalations. Every call has an agenda shared in advance and a written recap sent within 24 hours of the call. Never call a meeting for something that can be resolved in an email.

**Shared project tools (ADO, Jira, or similar)** are for story-level communication — acceptance criteria, technical clarifications, and feedback on specific work items. Client contacts who are in the tool can comment directly on work items. That's appropriate and encouraged for day-to-day collaboration.

**Phone or text** is reserved for genuine urgency — production down, critical blocker, emergency decision needed. If you're texting or calling a client, something serious is happening. Make sure it actually is.

**Do not use Teams DM or informal chat for business-critical communication** unless the client has explicitly established that as their preference. Chat is ephemeral and gets lost.

---

## Meeting Standards

Every client meeting follows the same discipline:

**Agenda in advance.** Send the agenda at least 24 hours before the meeting. For recurring ceremonies (sprint demo, backlog refinement), maintain a standing agenda that gets updated each cycle.

**Start and end on time.** Respect the client's calendar. If the conversation is running long, name it: "We have five minutes left — should we schedule time to finish this?"

**Cameras on by default.** For video calls with clients, cameras on is the expectation unless the client has indicated otherwise. It signals engagement.

**Named next steps.** Every meeting ends with a summary of decisions made and next steps, with a named owner and a date for each item. Send the written recap within 24 hours.

**No dead air on important items.** If a decision was needed and the client hasn't responded, follow up after 48 hours. If it's blocking work, escalate the urgency in your message.

---

## Tone and Language

**Professional but not stiff.** We're a partner, not a vendor. Write and speak like a trusted colleague who happens to be an expert in software delivery.

**Plain English.** Avoid internal jargon (ADO, sprint poker, DoD, story points) without explanation. When you need to use a technical term, define it briefly in context. Clients shouldn't feel like they need a decoder ring to follow our communications.

**Own the situation.** When something goes wrong, don't hedge or deflect. "We underestimated the complexity of the integration, and here's what we're doing to correct it" is better than "the timeline was impacted by unforeseen technical factors." One builds trust; the other reads like a press release.

**Positive framing is not spin.** Be direct about problems — don't bury them in good news. But do lead with context: the client needs to understand what's happening before they can evaluate the impact.

## Scope and Change Requests

Clients will ask for things that aren't in scope. This is normal. The way we handle it determines whether it creates friction or strengthens the relationship.

When a client requests something outside the SOW:

1. Acknowledge the request positively — "That makes sense, let me assess the impact."
2. Don't say yes or no on the spot. Assess the effort and impact first.
3. Document the request in writing — a quick email confirming what was asked.
4. Return with a clear answer: in scope, out of scope with a change order estimate, or a suggestion for how to accommodate it within existing scope by de-prioritizing something else.

Never let a scope request disappear. Either it gets acted on or it gets explicitly declined with a reason.

---

## Escalation Protocol

When a client is unhappy, escalating it fast is almost always the right move. Trying to manage a frustrated client at the DM level while hiding it from leadership makes things worse.

**Signals that it's time to escalate to the Portfolio Manager:**

- A client exec has expressed dissatisfaction directly or via their PM
- The client has asked for a DM change
- A formal complaint has been received
- The client is unresponsive for more than a week without explanation
- You're in a standoff over scope or budget that isn't moving

The Portfolio Manager can de-escalate conversations that have become adversarial at the DM level. Involve them early, not as a last resort.

---

## Client Onboarding to Tools

When a client needs access to shared tools (ADO boards, Notion pages, SharePoint folders), the DM is responsible for provisioning access and walking the client through what they'll see and how to use it.

First-time access always comes with a short orientation — either a 15-minute video call or a written guide, depending on client preference. Don't just throw a link at them and assume they'll figure it out.

---

## Offboarding and Relationship Close

When an engagement ends, the DM conducts a formal closeout conversation with the client (see [Project Lifecycle](../delivery/project-lifecycle.md)). Even if the client relationship is ending, leave cleanly — archived documentation, transferred access, and a genuine conversation about what went well and what could have been better.

How we end engagements affects whether clients refer us to others and whether they come back.
